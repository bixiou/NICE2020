# Referee report — *International Transfers or Differentiated Carbon Prices?*

Submitted to the *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management*. Second report, written 28 September 2026 on the revised version of `paper.tex` (main text about 5,630 words, three main-text exhibits, online appendix). It supersedes the first report.

**Recommendation: major revision.**

## Summary

The paper argues that income-differentiated national carbon prices, as proposed by the IMF, Wolfram et al. (2025), Banerjee, Duflo and Greenstone (2025) and Equal Right, are equivalent to a uniform price combined with an allocation of tradable rights. It makes three claims:

1. **Equivalence.** To first order, the equivalent allocation is grandfathering on the emissions the schedule induces. Exactly, it is that allocation minus a Harberger term divided by the price, and in a dynamic setting each year is weighted by the discounted price.
2. **Dominance.** With that allocation the uniform price Pareto-dominates differentiation, so the surplus can be taken as lower emissions or higher consumption.
3. **One-way equivalence.** No non-negative price can compensate a low emitter as an equal per capita allocation would.

The quantification in NICE (179 countries) gives three sets of results:
- indifference curves for eight economies;
- equivalent rights for three proposals: replacing them cuts coalition emissions by about 5% (13% for Equal Right), or raises every member's consumption by 0.04–0.05% (0.17%);
- a new estimate of the transfers that differentiation hides, and a striking Equal Right result: with global pooling of revenue, differentiation leaves the lowest emitters worse off than a uniform price with equal per capita rights.

The revision is a clear improvement:
- The contribution is now stated precisely: the reverse map from prices to rights, its one-way nature, and the quantification.
- The paper engages honestly with the fact that the efficiency cost of differentiation is small.
- The Equal Right analysis is better explained, and its normative gloss is toned down.
- The minor issues of the first round are fixed.

The paper is clear, short, policy-relevant and transparent about its limits. My remaining concerns are about the new transfer estimate, which now carries much of the argument, and about the robustness of the numbers.

## Major comments

**1. The transfer estimate needs to be compared with the surplus, and computed consistently.** *(Most important.)*
- The abstract, introduction and conclusion now contrast modest efficiency gains with larger hidden transfers: about \$190 billion a year by 2050 under Banerjee et al. (gross flows, computed as p\*_t (E^A_it − E\*_it), the first-order implicit transfer of Proposition 1). This is the right object and a useful addition.
- **Two different objects in one paragraph.** Column τ of Table 2 is computed on the constant-ρ allocation of the table, while the annual figures are computed on grandfathered emissions. The two coincide in present value to first order, and they do here (−0.30% against −0.31% of GDP for the United States). But they differ year by year, because a constant-ρ allocation shifts rights over time. The paper should compute both from the same object, preferably the grandfathered one, or say explicitly why they differ.
- **Same units.** The claim that the hidden transfers are "larger" than the efficiency gains is made in different units: annual dollars against percentages of consumption. Please report the surplus and the gross transfers in present-value dollars, side by side.
- **Size relative to GDP.** In present value the hidden transfers are a few tenths of a percent of GDP at most (−0.31% for the United States, +0.30% for India). The theory section still says that "the distributional stakes of the allocation are large". "Several times larger than the efficiency gains, though small relative to GDP" would be more accurate.

**2. The contribution is clearer, but the theory remains thin.** The reframing helps, and the paper is right not to oversell Propositions 1 and 2. For a general field journal, one of the following would add depth:
- a result on when the second-order term matters, i.e. how the surplus scales with price dispersion (the text states "second order" but does not exploit it quantitatively);
- a treatment of the one-way bound in the dynamic, non-Hotelling case actually used in the numbers.

Neither is essential, but either would help against a referee who sees the propositions as textbook.

**3. The robustness of the quantitative results is untested.** These points from the first round remain, and they matter for the headline numbers:
- **Price paths.** All tiers are held until 2030 and then raised by 5% a year forever. The proposals foresee graduation into a uniform price. Since the surplus is second order in dispersion, a convergence scenario could shrink it considerably. At least one such scenario should be reported.
- **Abatement costs.** In NICE every country has the same abatement-rate curve. Heterogeneous marginal abatement costs are the standard source of gains from uniform pricing and would change both the equivalent rights and the surplus. This deserves at least a discussion.
- **Emissions basis.** Emissions are measured as consumption-based footprints in the runs, but the main text does not say so, and the proposals price territorial emissions.
- **Discounting and horizon.** A 3% discount rate and a 2025–2100 window. A sensitivity check on either would be cheap.

**4. The welfare variant is still more distracting than informative.** The main text now says clearly that the 27% cut of the welfare variant comes mostly from within-country redistribution through the carbon dividend. If so, reporting it in the main text invites confusion. I would confine it to the appendix, keeping in the main text only the fact that the allocations at π = 1 coincide across variants.

## Minor comments

1. The introduction no longer has a roadmap. JEEM does not require one, but a single sentence would help readers of a paper with two quite different quantitative designs.
2. Section 4 and Section 5 use different uniform prices and normalisations. The new sentence in Section 5.2 helps; a word in the Table 2 note that ρ is relative to the *coalition's* emissions per capita (not the world's, as in Table 1) would prevent misreading.
3. The label "τ (%)" is not self-explanatory in the table header. "Transfer (% GDP)" would be clearer.
4. The claim that "85% of respondents worldwide state that climate policy at the global level is needed" is used against a "supposed taste for national sovereignty". Stated support for global policy does not directly measure acceptance of sovereignty costs, so the sentence could be softened.
5. The Equal Right paragraph now gives three ratios (0.64, 0.85, 0.75) in quick succession. A small table or a single sentence restating which applies to which allocation would help.
6. The sentence on Mongolia is now a footnote, which is appropriate; Appendix B could state that the losers are exactly those with negative marginal damages.

## Assessment

The paper has improved substantially and is close to a publishable JEEM contribution: a clean, usable equivalence, a clear statement of what it does and does not show, and an honest discussion of the size of the efficiency gains.

The main outstanding issues are:
- presenting the transfer estimate consistently and in the same units as the surplus, since the paper's central message now rests on the comparison between the two;
- the robustness of the numbers to the assumed price paths, in particular a scenario in which prices converge;
- a clear statement of the emissions basis.

None requires new modelling beyond runs the authors can already do. This is a manageable revision.

**Recommendation: major revision**, bordering on minor if the transfer comparison is made in common units and a convergence scenario is reported.
