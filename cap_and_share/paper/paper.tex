\begin{abstract}
\noindent Recent proposals for international carbon pricing differentiate national carbon prices by income. The usual justification is that cross-border transfers are politically infeasible. We show that differentiated prices are themselves equivalent to transfers. Any schedule of national prices is distributionally equivalent to a uniform price combined with a certain allocation of emission rights, equal to each country's emissions under the schedule at the first order. 
In a dynamic setting, the rights are weighted by the discounted carbon price, and under Hotelling pricing they reduce to a carbon budget. Because abatement costs are convex, this allocation leaves every country weakly better off. % at the same global cap. 
The uniform price with equivalent rights therefore Pareto-dominates differentiation. We quantify the equivalence in NICE2020, an integrated assessment model with 179 countries and within-country income deciles. %The closed-form prediction explains 95--99\% of the cross-country variance in the numerically solved equivalent rights. 
Replacing the schedules of \citet{parry_proposal_2021}, \citet{wolfram_building_2025} or \citet{banerjee_grand_2025} with a uniform price and equivalent rights would cut coalition emissions by 9--10\% while keeping every member's consumption unchanged. Alternatively, holding emissions fixed, it would raise every member's consumption. We also review departures from standard assumptions 
that could justify price differentiation and argue that they are not compelling. %These gains are small at the price dispersion of current proposals and grow steeply with dispersion. The substantive difference between the two regimes is therefore less their efficiency than whether transfers are explicit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

International climate policy is stuck on a distributional question: who should pay for abatement? The Paris Agreement endorses common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, and it commits developed countries to financing climate action in developing ones. It does not translate these principles into numbers, and national contributions remain far short of its temperature goal \citep{unep_emissions_2025}.

A prominent recent answer is to coordinate carbon prices while differentiating them by income. The IMF proposes an international price floor of \$75, \$50 and \$25/tCO$_2$ for high-, upper-middle- and lower-income countries \citep{parry_proposal_2021}. \citet{wolfram_building_2025} propose a climate coalition built on the same tiers. \citet{banerjee_grand_2025} propose a ``grand bargain'' in which low- and middle-income countries adopt prices of \$10 to \$50/t in exchange for compensation of climate damages. In all three proposals, carbon revenues are retained domestically. Differentiation is meant to deliver equity without the cross-border transfers that are deemed politically infeasible \citep{parry_proposal_2021,bauer_quantification_2020}.

Textbook theory prescribes something else: a uniform price for efficiency, and lump-sum transfers for equity \citep{aldy_promise_2012}. Without transfers, the efficient allocation of abatement generally involves different marginal costs across countries \citep{chichilnisky_who_1994,shiell_equity_2003}. Differentiated prices are then a second-best substitute for transfers. The case for differentiation therefore rests on the premise that transfers are politically infeasible.

This paper argues that the premise does not rest on solid ground. Majority acceptance of an international carbon price financing North-to-South transfers is found in all 23 surveyed countries \citep{fabre_majority_2025,fabre_public_2025}. % ICS
Furthermore, a uniform price can achieve efficiency while remaining %emission rights can be designed to be 
distributionally equivalent to differentiated prices. We make the claim precise in three results.

First, any schedule of differentiated autarky prices is distributionally equivalent to a uniform price combined with a specific allocation of tradable emission rights. For a country whose autarky price is equal to the uniform price, that allocation gives exactly the emissions it would have had under autarky: this is the first-order approximation of equivalent rights. A country allowed a lower price than the global average receives, in the equivalent uniform-price regime, additional rights to its higher emissions. Conversely, a positive price gap relative to the global average is equivalent to less emission rights than in autarky. % TODO? improve this sentence?
In a static setting, we derive %the exact equivalent allocation and 
a second-order formula of the equivalent allocation in terms of the price gap and the price elasticity of emissions (Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}).

Second, the uniform price with equivalent allocation results in lower global emissions compared to differentiation, thereby making every country better off (Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance}). This is the familiar gains-from-trade logic of emissions markets \citep{montgomery_markets_1972} and the international analogue of production efficiency \citep{diamond_optimal_1971}. Its implication for the policy debate is less familiar: any price-differentiation agreement can be replaced by a uniform price with transfers such that no country loses. The resulting surplus can take the form of reduced emissions or increased consumption. % at unchanged consumption or of higher consumption at unchanged emissions.

Third, in a dynamic setting the equivalence holds in present value, with each year's rights weighted by the discounted carbon price (Proposition~\ref{prop:dynamic}). When the carbon price follows a Hotelling path, only the cumulative carbon budget matters.

We then quantify the equivalence in NICE2020 \citep{young-brun_within-country_2025}, an integrated assessment model with 179 countries and ten income deciles per country. %We run two exercises.

First, we compare welfare under autarky and under a global uniform price for eight diverse economies, taking global emissions as fixed at the level implied by the uniform price, itself chosen to limit global warming at 1.8\textdegree{}C. For each level of autarky price and each level of emission rights, we compute the welfare difference between the uniform price and autarky, and trace the indifference curve between the two. 
This illustrates that while there is an equivalent uniform-price allocation for each differentiated price schedule, the converse is not true: above a certain level of emission rights, there is no non-negative price that would make a country prefer autarky. For example, Nigeria would be better off under a uniform price as long as its emission rights per capita are at least 15\% of the global average, even compared to an autarky price of zero.

Second, we compute the equivalent rights for prominent differentiated prices proposals. %The closed-form prediction explains 95--99\% of the cross-country variance in the equivalent rights solved numerically in general equilibrium. 
Granting all members their equivalent rights cuts coalition emissions by 9\% \citep{banerjee_grand_2025}, 10\% \citep{wolfram_building_2025}, % same rounded figures for A and B
and 17\% \citep{equal_right_climate_2023} while raising each member's welfare by the same factor.

\paragraph{Related literature.} The paper contributes to four strands of literature.

The first studies permit allocation. Initial allocations do not affect the efficiency of a cap-and-trade \citep{montgomery_markets_1972,hahn_effect_2011}. We invert this independence property: we ask which allocation reproduces the distributional outcome of a given price instrument, and derive the answer as a closed form equation.

The second concerns equity and efficiency in international climate policy without transfers \citep{chichilnisky_who_1994,shiell_equity_2003,anthoff_inequality_2019}, and the second-best rationales for differentiated carbon prices: differences in discount rates \citep{anthoff_differentiated_2026}, pre-existing distortions and terms-of-trade motives \citep{babiker_is_2004,babiker_climate_2005,boeters_optimally_2014}, and the ``efficiency--sovereignty'' trade-off \citep{bauer_quantification_2020,bekkers_comparing_2022}. Our result speaks to the last of these. It shows that the sovereignty argument cannot rest on the need to avoid transfers, since differentiation entails transfers of the same size.

\section{The equivalence between differentiated prices and emission rights}
\label{sec:theory}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Static setting}

Consider countries $i=1,\dots,I$ with populations $n_i$, in a single period. All variables are per capita unless noted. Country $i$ emits $e_i$ at abatement cost $a_i(e_i)$, which is convex in $e_i$. Facing a carbon price $p$, firms and households emit $e_i(p)$,  optimising by setting the marginal abatement cost equal to the price: $\frac{da_i}{de}(e_i) = -p$. Consumption is
\begin{equation}
  c_i = y_i - a_i(e_i) + t_i,
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is potential output and $t_i$ a net international transfer. Welfare is increasing in $c_i$ and decreasing in global emissions $E=\sum_j n_j e_j$ through climate damages.

We compare regimes that deliver the same global emissions $E^*$.
%The question is therefore not what the global target should be, but how a given target is shared. 
Damages are identical across the regimes compared, and a country is better off in one regime than in another if and only if its consumption is higher.

\paragraph{Autarky.} Country $i$ applies a domestic price $p_i$, keeps its revenue and receives no transfer. It emits $e^A_i \equiv e_i(p_i)$, and global emissions are $E^*$.% $E^A=\sum_i n_i e^A_i$.

\paragraph{Uniform price.} The same countries apply a uniform price $\bar p$ and trade emission rights under a cap equal to $E^*$. % $E^A$. 
Country $i$ receives rights $r_i$ per capita. At the market-clearing price, $\sum_i n_i e_i(\bar p) = E^*$, % E^A
and $i$ emits $\bar e_i \equiv e_i(\bar p)$. Its net transfer is the value of its sales minus purchase of rights, $t_i=(r_i-\bar e_i)\bar p$. 
%These transfers sum to zero whenever $\sum_i n_i r_i = E^A$.

We write $\pi_i \equiv p_i/\bar p$ for country $i$'s relative price, and $\varepsilon_i$ for the price elasticity of its emissions at $\bar p$: $\varepsilon_i = -\frac{de_i/\bar{e_i}}{dp/\bar{p}}$. Define the \emph{convexity gap}
\begin{equation}
  B_i \equiv a_i(e^A_i) - a_i(\bar e_i) - a_i'(\bar e_i)\,(e^A_i-\bar e_i) \;\geq\; 0,
  \label{eq:bregman}
\end{equation}
which is the Harberger triangle % TODO: is it really a Harberger triangle? It seems it doesn't have a triangle shape
between the country's marginal abatement cost curve and the uniform price, over the emissions it gives up by moving from $p_i$ to $\bar p$. The gap is nonnegative by convexity, and strictly positive if $a_i$ is strictly convex and $p_i\neq\bar p$.

% TODO consider removing all indices i from the formulas/notations: do it only if it makes sense
\begin{proposition}[Equivalent rights]\label{prop:equiv}
Country $i$ is exactly as well off under the uniform-price regime as under the differentiated regime if and only if it receives
\begin{equation}
  r^{\mathrm{eq}}_i \;=\; e^A_i - \frac{B_i}{\bar p}.
  \label{eq:req_exact}
\end{equation}
% TODO: replace r^{\mathrm{eq}} by r^* throughout (if it is consistent with the other notations)
To first order in the price gap, $r^{\mathrm{eq}}_i \approx e^A_i$. To second order, with $\varepsilon_i$ evaluated at $\bar p$,
\begin{equation}
  r^{\mathrm{eq}}_i \;\approx\; e^A_i - \frac{(e^A_i-\bar e_i)^2}{2\,\varepsilon_i\,\bar e_i}
  \;\approx\; \bar e_i\Bigl[\,1+\varepsilon_i(1-\pi_i)-\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_i(1-\pi_i)^2\Bigr].
  \label{eq:req_second}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equating $y_i-a_i(\bar e_i)+(r_i-\bar e_i)\bar p$ with $y_i-a_i(e^A_i)$ gives $r_i=\bar e_i+[a_i(\bar e_i)-a_i(e^A_i)]/\bar p$. Substituting $a_i'(\bar e_i)=-\bar p$ into \eqref{eq:bregman} yields \eqref{eq:req_exact}. For \eqref{eq:req_second}, note that $a_i''(\bar e_i)=-\,\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}e=\bar p/(\varepsilon_i\bar e_i)$, so that $B_i\approx \tfrac12\,\bar p\,(e^A_i-\bar e_i)^2/(\varepsilon_i\bar e_i)$. The second expression then follows from $e^A_i-\bar e_i\approx\varepsilon_i\bar e_i(1-\pi_i)$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} shows that a differentiated price is a transfer paid in kind. Relative to the uniform price, a country allowed a lower price ($\pi_i<1$) keeps emissions $e^A_i>\bar e_i$. To first order, the uniform-price regime replicates its situation by granting it exactly those emissions as rights, part of which it then sells, receiving $(e^A_i-\bar e_i)\bar p$. Conversely, a country asked for a higher price ($\pi_i>1$) is equivalent to one that buys rights. The size of the implicit transfer grows with the price gap, the country's emissions and the elasticity of those emissions. The same formula, read in reverse, converts any allocation of rights into an equivalent autarky price.

% E^A => E^*?
It is convenient to express the allocation as a multiple of an equal per capita share of the cap, $r_i=\rho_i E^A/N$, where $N=\sum_i n_i$. The first-order prediction is then
\begin{equation}
  \hat\rho_i \;=\; \frac{e^A_i}{E^A/N},
  \label{eq:rhohat_static}
\end{equation}
country $i$'s per capita emissions under the differentiated schedule relative to the average. A differentiated schedule is thus, to first order, a grandfathering rule, applied to the emissions that the schedule itself induces.

\begin{proposition}[Dominance of the uniform price]\label{prop:dominance}
Allocate rights $r_i=e^A_i$ to every country. This allocation (i) is feasible at the cap of the differentiated regime, $\sum_i n_i r_i=E^A$; (ii) leaves every country weakly better off than under the differentiated regime, strictly so if $a_i$ is strictly convex and $p_i\neq\bar p$; and (iii) leaves a surplus of $\sum_i n_i B_i\geq 0$. Expressed in emissions, the cap can be tightened by $\sum_i n_i B_i/\bar p$ tonnes with no country worse off than under differentiated prices.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Part (i) holds by construction. For part (ii), the consumption gain of country $i$ is $[a_i(e^A_i)-a_i(\bar e_i)]+(e^A_i-\bar e_i)\bar p=B_i\geq0$. For part (iii), sum these gains. Moreover, $e^A_i-r^{\mathrm{eq}}_i=B_i/\bar p$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, so $E^A-\sum_i n_i r^{\mathrm{eq}}_i=\sum_i n_i B_i/\bar p$.
\end{proof}

A country that is allowed a lower price saves on abatement costs, but at a diminishing rate. Under the uniform price, the same country can abate up to $\bar p$ and sell the freed rights at $\bar p$, which is always at least as good a deal. By Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, the surplus is of second order in the price gaps, whereas the implicit transfers are of first order. The efficiency argument for a uniform price is therefore robust but quantitatively modest when prices are not very dispersed, whereas the distributional stakes of the allocation are large. Section~\ref{sec:results} confirms both features numerically.

Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance} is a first-best statement. It abstracts from the rationales for differentiation that rest on other distortions, such as heterogeneous discount rates \citep{anthoff_differentiated_2026}, pre-existing taxes, terms-of-trade effects and leakage \citep{babiker_is_2004,babiker_climate_2005,boeters_optimally_2014}. None of these requires the absence of transfers. %, and our result says nothing about them. 
What it questions is the argument that differentiation is needed because transfers would be infeasible: differentiation involves the same distributive outcome as well chosen transfers, it only makes them implicit.

\subsection{Dynamic setting}
\label{sec:dynamic}

With several periods, prices $p_{it}$, rights $r_{it}$ and emissions become trajectories, and $\beta_t$ denotes the discount factor. Welfare equivalence now requires equal net present values of consumption.

\begin{proposition}[Dynamic equivalence]\label{prop:dynamic}
Suppose abatement costs are separable over time. To first order, country $i$ is indifferent between the differentiated regime and the uniform price path $(\bar p_t)$ with rights $(r_{it})$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \sum_t \beta_t\,\bar p_t\,\bigl(r_{it}-e^A_{it}\bigr) \;=\; 0.
  \label{eq:dyn_equiv}
\end{equation}
If rights are a constant multiple of an equal per capita share of the cap, $r_{it}=\rho_i\,\bar E^A_t$ with $\bar E^A_t=E^A_t/N_t$, then % TODO: use \bar for world average and * for uniform price throughout. If you find that * conflicts with other notations or is unsuited, use ^U instead
\begin{equation}
  \hat\rho_i \;=\; \frac{\sum_t \beta_t\,\bar p_t\,e^A_{it}}{\sum_t \beta_t\,\bar p_t\,\bar E^A_t}.% TODO \hat\rho_i is not defined
  \label{eq:rhohat_dyn}
\end{equation}
If, in addition, the discounted price $\beta_t\bar p_t$ is constant over time (Hotelling), then $\hat\rho_i$ is the ratio of country $i$'s cumulative per capita emissions under the schedule to the equal per capita share of the cumulative budget.
\end{proposition}
% TODO: add to the proposition the formula of rho for Hotelling

\begin{proof}
Apply the first-order argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} period by period and sum the discounted consumption differences.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:dyn_equiv} shows that timing matters. A tonne of rights is worth more in a period of high prices, so the allocation depends on the whole price path, and a country's cumulative carbon budget does not by itself determine its welfare. 
Countries whose emissions fall quickly under the schedule need fewer rights than their cumulative share suggests, to the extent that the price is high when their emissions are low. %Conversely, countries whose emissions keep rising need more rights. 
Only under a Hotelling price path, the natural benchmark with a fixed carbon budget and efficient capital markets, does the price weighting drop out.

Equation~\eqref{eq:rhohat_dyn} is the first-order approximation that we take to the model. It ignores three channels that NICE2020 includes: the higher-order terms due to the convexity of abatement costs, transfers that affect capital accumulation, as well as the incidence of prices and transfers within countries. %, and the general-equilibrium adjustment of the uniform price when allocations change. How much these channels matter is the empirical question of Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{NICE2020}
% TODO? dissolve this section into others (including the appendix)?

We use NICE2020 \citep{young-brun_within-country_2025}, a recursive-dynamic integrated assessment model in the RICE tradition. It represents 179 countries, each divided into ten income deciles, and is coupled to the FaIR climate model. Each country has its own damage function and its own marginal abatement cost curve, which reaches a backstop price at full abatement. % TODO: are these really country-specific?
The distribution of both carbon costs and climate damages across deciles follows estimated income elasticities.

Three features %of the model matter 
for our purpose. First, domestic carbon revenues are recycled within each country in proportion to $c^{\eta}$, the inverse of marginal utility. % TODO? remove this if we cut the EDE results? If so, say we don't use the within-country inequality of the model
This rule roughly preserves within-country inequality, so our comparisons isolate between-country effects rather than the redistributive effect of recycling carbon revenue through, say, a per capita dividend. Second, international transfers enter national income and therefore affect investment and subsequent growth. Third, we allow abatement beyond 100\% of emissions, which represents carbon dioxide removal, by extrapolating the cost curve beyond the backstop. Without this extension the model fails under the steep price paths explored below.

We use two values for maximization purposes: consumption and welfare. Welfare is measured as the net present value over 2030--2100, discounted at 3\% a year, of equally-distributed-equivalent (EDE) consumption (with inequality aversion $\eta=1.5$). EDE consumption reflects the consumption level in a world with complete equality equivalent to a given distribution of consumption; it is a way to express welfare in monetary terms. Online Appendix~\ref{app:model} gives further details.

\subsection{Exercise 1: single-country indifference curves}

The benchmark is a uniform price path $p^*_t$ consistent with 1.8\textdegree C of warming. It is \$42.6/t in 2030, \$206/t in 2050 and \$471/t in 2100. % TODO and grows at a constant rate xx?
For a given country $i$, we compare two scenarios with the same global emission path.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Autarky}: country $i$ applies $\pi_i p^*_t$ and recycles its revenue domestically. The rest of the world applies a uniform price that we solve for year by year, %with a Newton step and a bisection fallback, 
  so that global emissions stay on the 1.8\textdegree C path. % within 0.1\%.
  \item \emph{Uniform price}: all countries apply $p^*_t$. Country $i$ receives rights $\rho_i$ times its population share of global emissions, % TODO: at time t? or the population share is evaluated at another date?
  and the remaining rights go to the rest of the world in proportion to its members' emissions.
\end{itemize}

A grid over $(\pi_i,\rho_i)$ gives the welfare difference between the two scenarios, % TODO: check that we use welfare, not conso
and hence country $i$'s indifference curve. We run the grid for eight entities: the United States, Russia, China, Turkey, the European Union (EU27), India, Nigeria and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). 

\subsection{Exercise 2: equivalent rights for price proposals}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

Differentiating carbon prices by income is increasingly proposed as a way to reconcile carbon pricing with equity. This paper questions the premise of differentiation that cross-border transfers are infeasible. A differentiated schedule is equivalent, to first order, to a uniform price with tradable rights that grandfather each country on the emissions the schedule itself induces. These are transfers of the same first-order size, only paid in (excess or forgone) abatement rather than in money. 
Moreover, since abatement costs are convex, the explicit version leaves every country weakly better off at the same cap.

% The quantitative analysis supports each step of the argument. The closed-form allocation explains nearly all of the cross-country variation in the equivalent rights solved in a model with capital accumulation, within-country inequality and general-equilibrium price adjustment. 
Replacing the differentiated prices of \citet{wolfram_building_2025} or \citet{banerjee_grand_2025} with a uniform price at equivalent rights would reduce future global emissions by 10\% % TODO: correct figure
while preserving welfare in every country.

Two implications follow for negotiations. First, the equivalence formula provides an exchange rate between a price and emission rights, allowing parties to shift the discussion from one notion to the other as needed. %can negotiate over one and implement the other, so that equity and efficiency are separated as the textbook recommends. 
Second, the value of national sovereignty invoked to dismiss an international cap-and-trade should be balanced with the efficiency gains that an international commitment would enable. 
% Second, since the implicit and explicit transfers are of the same size, the political objection to transfers must be an objection to their visibility, not to their magnitude. %Whether visibility helps or hinders an agreement is an open empirical question. 
Furthermore, survey evidence shows %that large explicit North-to-South transfers are actually popular even in high-income countries and indicates 
that 85\% of worldwide respondents state that climate policy at the \textit{global} level is needed, at odd with the supposed taste for national sovereignty \citep{fabre_majority_2025}. Finally, robust majority acceptance of an international cap-and-trade funding equal per capita cash transfers indicates that most people are ready with large North-to-South transfers unattainable with differentiated autarky prices.

The analysis has limitations. NICE2020 has no trade, so it cannot capture leakage or the terms-of-trade motives for differentiation. It has no sectoral detail, which forced us to apply sectoral proposals economy-wide and ignores the country-specific costs of sectoral reallocation. It also assumes perfect enforcement of both regimes. The proposals' price paths after 2030 are our assumption. Extending the equivalence to settings with trade and different sectors, where a uniform price must coexist with border adjustments, is a natural next step to better inform climate negotiators.

\section{Model details}
\label{app:model}

\paragraph{Rest-of-world price in the autarky scenario.} When country $i$ applies $\pi_i p^*_t$, the rest of the world applies a price $p_{-i,t}$ such that global emissions follow the 1.8\textdegree C path. A linear first guess weights prices by emission shares, $p^*=\omega_i p_i+(1-\omega_i)p_{-i}$ with $\omega_i=e_i/E^*$. Because the marginal abatement cost curves are nonlinear and emission shares respond to the tax, this guess misses the cap. We therefore solve for $p_{-i,t}$ by Newton iterations on the gap between simulated and target global emissions, falling back on bisection where Newton stalls, until the relative error is below 0.1\%.

\paragraph{Rights of the rest of the world in the uniform-price scenario.} Country $i$ receives $\rho_i$ times its population share of the global budget. The remainder is distributed to other countries in proportion to their emissions, which keeps total rights equal to the cap for any $\rho_i$.

\paragraph{Abatement beyond 100\%.} Standard implementations constrain the abatement rate to $\mu\in[0,1]$. Under high autarky prices this constraint binds and the solver fails. We extrapolate the cost function beyond $\mu=1$, which represents carbon dioxide removal at rising marginal cost.

\paragraph{Viability flags.} We flag grid points at which (i) the rest-of-world price would have to be negative to keep the global path ($\omega_i\pi_i>1$), and (ii) country $i$'s rights would exceed the global budget, leaving the rest of the world with negative rights (crosses in Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps}).

\section{Solves and sharing rules}
\label{app:variants}

\paragraph{Isolated and joint solves.} The two solves agree on the aggregates. Taking the equivalent allocation at face value cuts the Wolfram et al.\ club's emissions by 6.9\% (isolated) or 6.6\% (joint), and the Banerjee et al.\ club's by 6.7\% or 6.4\%. The implied 2100 temperatures differ by less than 0.01\textdegree C. They differ on the distribution, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

In the joint solve, the aggregate level of rights is held at the isolated solve's value, and only the spread across countries is solved. That spread is brought down to 0.0017\% (Wolfram et al.) and 0.0019\% (Banerjee et al.) of each country's consumption. The residual population-weighted mean gap, $+0.034\%$ and $+0.048\%$, is the aggregate surplus at that level rather than a convergence failure.

\paragraph{Variant 2: maximin.} The surplus is shared so as to maximise the smallest relative consumption gain of any member against the proposal. The optimisation is done on consumption rather than on rights, because returning rights loosens the cap and lowers the club price, and a net seller's revenue $(r_i-e_i)p$ falls with the price. The search runs over the whole allocation and starts from both the equivalent allocation and the grandfathered one of Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance}, which is known to be Pareto-improving. We keep whichever start ends with the higher floor.

\paragraph{Variant 3: marginal utility.} The surplus is shared in proportion to $n_i c_i^{-\eta}$, with $c_i$ the member's EDE consumption under the proposal. This rule yields the largest inequality-averse gain ($+0.24\%$ for Banerjee et al.\ under the joint solve), but 24 of 179 members end below their proposal consumption.

\paragraph{Variant 4: uniform scaling.} All rights are multiplied by one common factor. The extra rights go to the largest holders, and the lower club price devalues the rights of small, generously endowed net sellers. Under this rule 7 of 47 Wolfram et al.\ members and 43 of 179 Banerjee et al.\ members lose, and the global EDE gain for Wolfram et al.\ is slightly negative ($-0.002\%$).

\section{Consumption and EDE targets, and the Equal Right schedules}
\label{app:ede}

Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_targets_er5_losers} reports equivalent allocations solved on EDE and on mean consumption, for the two proposals and for the Equal Right schedule at 5\% a year. The two measures can move in opposite directions for the same country, for a within-country reason. Carbon costs fall on deciles in proportion to income raised to an elasticity below one, which is regressive. Revenue is returned in proportion to $c^{\eta}$, which is regressive too. More domestic pricing therefore widens within-country inequality. This is why a member held exactly at its proposal consumption can still gain or lose on the EDE, and why we target mean consumption, which answers the between-country question of this paper directly.

Equal Right, on its own 16.4\%-a-year escalation path, pushes most members to full abatement by 2050 and reaches 1.48\textdegree C of warming. Its club price is far above the other schedules', and the ranking of EDE- and consumption-equivalent allocations reverses. Solved country by country, the United States' allocation even turns negative ($-0.24$): it would pay a net fee for its rights and still prefer the club price to its own. Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_er} reports both Equal Right paths.
