\begin{abstract}
\noindent Recent proposals for international carbon pricing differentiate national carbon prices by income. The usual justification is that cross-border transfers are politically infeasible. But differentiated prices are themselves equivalent to transfers. We show that any schedule of national prices is distributionally equivalent to a uniform price combined with a certain allocation of tradable emission rights, equal at the first order to each country's emissions under price differentiation. 
In a dynamic setting, the equivalent rights are weighted by the discounted carbon price, and under Hotelling pricing they reduce to a carbon budget. Because abatement costs are convex, this allocation leaves every country weakly better off, so the uniform price with equivalent rights Pareto-dominates differentiation. %The converse does not hold: no schedule of non-negative prices can deliver to a low emitter what a generous allocation of rights would.
We quantify the equivalence in NICE2020, an integrated assessment model with 179 countries. % The closed-form allocation matches the simulated one within 6\% when a country's price equals the uniform price.
Replacing the schedules of \citet{parry_proposal_2021}, \citet{wolfram_building_2025} or \citet{banerjee_grand_2025} with a uniform price and equivalent rights would cut the coalition's emissions by about 5\% while leaving every member as well off. Alternatively, holding emissions fixed, it would raise every member's consumption. We also review the departures from standard assumptions
that could justify price differentiation and argue that they are not compelling. %These gains are small at the price dispersion of current proposals and grow steeply with dispersion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

International climate policy is stuck on a distributional question: who should obtain the rights to emit? The Paris Agreement endorses common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, and it commits developed countries to financing climate action in developing ones. It does not translate these principles into numbers, and national contributions remain far short of its temperature goal \citep{unep_emissions_2025}.

A prominent recent answer is to coordinate carbon prices while differentiating them by income. The IMF proposes an international price floor of \$75, \$50 and \$25/tCO$_2$ for high-, upper middle- and lower-income countries \citep{parry_proposal_2021}. \citet{wolfram_building_2025} propose a climate coalition built on the same tiers. \citet{banerjee_grand_2025} propose a ``grand bargain'' in which low-, and middle-income countries adopt prices of \$10 to \$50/t in exchange for the compensation of their climate damages. In all three proposals, carbon revenues are retained domestically. Differentiation is meant to deliver equity without the cross-border transfers that are deemed politically infeasible \citep{parry_proposal_2021}.

Textbook theory prescribes something else: a uniform price for efficiency, and lump-sum transfers for equity \citep{aldy_promise_2012}. Differentiated prices are a second-best substitute for transfers, and the case for income-based differentiation therefore rests on the premise that transfers are infeasible. This paper argues that the premise does not rest on solid ground. Survey evidence already challenges it: majorities accept an international carbon price financing North--South transfers in all 23 surveyed countries \citep{fabre_majority_2025,fabre_public_2025}. % ICS
Furthermore, a uniform price can achieve efficiency while remaining 
distributionally equivalent to differentiated prices. We make the claim precise in three results.

% For a country whose autarky price is equal to the uniform price, that allocation gives exactly the emissions it would have had under autarky: this is the first-order approximation of equivalent rights. A country allowed a lower price than the global average receives, in the equivalent uniform-price regime, additional rights to its higher emissions. 
First, any schedule of differentiated autarky prices is distributionally equivalent to a uniform price combined with a specific allocation of tradable emission rights. To first order, this allocation grants each country the emissions it has under price differentiation. A country allowed a price below the uniform price therefore receives more rights than it emits at the uniform price, and sells the difference. %; a country asked for a higher price receives fewer, and buys the difference. %The implicit costs and benefits implied by differentiation are thus of the same first-order size as those of an explicit allocation of rights. 
Conversely, a positive price gap is equivalent to less emission rights than in autarky.

Second, granting each country its emissions under the schedule leaves every country weakly better off under the uniform price than under differentiation (Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance}). This is the familiar gains-from-trade logic of emissions markets \citep{montgomery_markets_1972} and the international analogue of production efficiency \citep{diamond_optimal_1971}. Its implication for the policy debate is less familiar: any price-differentiation agreement can be replaced by a uniform price with an allocation of rights such that no country loses. The resulting surplus can take the form of reduced emissions or of increased consumption.

Third, in a dynamic setting the equivalence holds in present value, with each year's rights weighted by the discounted carbon price (Proposition~\ref{prop:dynamic}). When the carbon price follows a Hotelling path, the equivalent allocation is a carbon budget: each country's cumulative emissions under price differentiation.

\section{Why differentiate carbon prices?}
\label{sec:arguments}
% ============================================================

With complete markets and no other distortion, % and lump-sum international transfers, 
efficiency requires a uniform carbon price, and equity should be addressed via lump-sum transfers \citep{sandmo_global_2007}. %: uniform Pigouvian taxation is optimal precisely when such transfers are available, and a uniform price is what our equivalence restores, with the transfers made explicit as rights. %Without transfers, the efficient allocation of abatement generally involves different marginal costs across countries, and differentiated prices become a second-best instrument for equity \citep{chichilnisky_who_1994,shiell_equity_2003,dautume_should_2016,anthoff_inequality_2019}. 
Four departures from standard assumptions have been invoked to justify differentiating carbon prices across countries or sectors: divergent discount rates, pre-existing distortive taxes, market power in trade, and constraints on cross-country transfers. We review them in turn.

First, \citet{anthoff_differentiated_2026} show that equalising carbon prices across countries is inefficient in case of `cross-country differences in discount rates (or interest rates)'', which arise from inefficiencies in the allocation of capital. By Hotelling's argument, each country's carbon price should grow at its own discount rate, so prices cannot be equal at all dates.\footnote{Differentiated prices may then be optimal in a second-best world, but the first best is in principle attainable by correcting the capital-market imperfections themselves.} The authors stress, however, that their result cannot say whether trade in emission permits from a given allocation would raise or lower welfare, as this depends on how permit trade interacts with the capital-market frictions, which no model has yet specified.

Second, pre-existing tax distortions can be mitigated by differentiating carbon prices across sectors or countries \citep{babiker_is_2004,boeters_optimally_2014}. For example, if aviation remains under-taxed, a higher carbon price on aviation than on other sectors improves welfare. For this reason (applied to countries), \citet{babiker_is_2004} show that a uniform price may not always be beneficial to the countries that take part in it. However, \citet{dautume_should_2016} show that a uniform price remains optimal if the other tax (such as a gasoline tax) addresses a local externality or if public goods have to be funded by distortive taxation. %Optimality is restored if countries are allowed to trade permits but firms can only trade them domestically (thereby preventing a uniform price).

Third, large countries have market power in world markets, which gives them a terms-of-trade motive to differentiate \citep{babiker_climate_2005,boeters_optimally_2014}. Take China, a large oil importer and manufacturing exporter. 
By taxing oil more heavily than other goods, China would depress world oil demand and hence the oil price, improving its terms of trade. Similarly, it has an interest in setting a higher carbon price on manufactured goods, to raise the price of its exports (to the extent the carbon price paid by importers exceeds the loss of exports due to higher prices).

Fourth, and most commonly, differentiated prices are justified by the assertion that international transfers are politically infeasible \citep{parry_proposal_2021,young-brun_within-country_2025}. \citet{bauer_quantification_2020} describe an ``efficiency--sovereignty'' trade-off in climate policy: either carbon prices are differentiated and efficiency is lost, or a uniform price with transfers is applied, hampering national sovereignty. This reasoning assumes that international transfers constitute a stronger infringement on sovereignty than coordinated price differentiation \citep[see also][]{bekkers_comparing_2022}. Because transfers are deemed infeasible for they infringe sovereignty, differentiated prices are viewed as a necessary second-best solution.

The first three rationales have an efficiency basis and do not rest on the absence of transfers. They also call for a differentiation that bears no systematic relation to income: by discount rate, by sector or by market power. This paper addresses the fourth rationale, the one invoked by differentiation proposals. We show that it cannot rest on the distributional implications of transfers, since differentiation entails implicit costs or benefits of the same size as the transfers that a uniform price with equivalent rights would involve. % TODO say instead why they're not compelling, as in prez

\subsection{Static setting}

Consider countries $i=1,\dots,I$ with populations $N_i$, % and total population $N=\sum_i N_i$, 
in a single period. Lower-case variables are per capita. Country $i$ emits $e_i$ at abatement cost $a_i(e_i)$, which is decreasing and convex in $e_i$. Facing a carbon price $p$, firms and households emit $e_i(p)$, optimising by equating the marginal abatement cost to (minus) the price: $a_i'(e_i(p)) = -p$. Consumption is
\begin{equation}
  c_i = y_i - a_i(e_i) + t_i,
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is potential output and $t_i$ a net international transfer. Welfare is increasing in $c_i$ and decreasing in global emissions through climate damages. 
% We work throughout in a total-utilitarian framework: a country's welfare is the sum of the welfare of its residents, $N_i\,u(c_i)$, and world welfare is $\sum_i N_i\,u(c_i)$. Within a single period this only scales each country's objective, so it does not affect the results below; it matters in the dynamic setting, where a country's population changes over time. % TODO put in dynamic setting

We compare two regimes that deliver the same global emissions $E=\sum_j N_j e_j$, whose world average per capita is $\bar e\equiv E/N$ (where $N$ is the world population).
%The question is therefore not what the global target should be, but how a given target is shared.
Damages are then identical across regimes, and a country is better off in one regime if and only if its consumption is higher.

\paragraph{Differentiated prices (autarky).} Country $i$ applies a domestic price $p_i$, keeps its revenue and receives no transfer. It emits $e^A_i \equiv e_i(p_i)$, and $\sum_i N_i e^A_i = E$.

\paragraph{Uniform price.} The same countries apply a uniform price $p^*$ and trade emission rights under a cap equal to $E$.
Country $i$ receives rights $r_i$ per capita. At the market-clearing price, $\sum_i N_i e_i(p^*) = E$,
and $i$ emits $e^*_i \equiv e_i(p^*)$. Its net transfer is the value of its sales minus its purchases of rights, $t_i=(r_i-e^*_i)\,p^*$.
%These transfers sum to zero whenever $\sum_i N_i r_i = E$.

  \label{eq:bregman}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Equivalent rights]\label{prop:equiv}
Denote $r^*_i$ the \textit{equivalent rights} that leave a country $i$ exactly as well off under the uniform-price regime as under the differentiated regime with a price $p_i$. Holding total emissions at $E$ and the carbon price at $p^*$, we have
% is exactly as well off under the uniform-price regime as under the differentiated regime if and only if it receives
\begin{equation}
  r^*_i \;=\; e^A_i - \frac{b_i}{p^*}. 
  \label{eq:req_exact}
\end{equation}
% TO DO: replace r^{\mathrm{eq}} by r^* throughout (if it is consistent with the other notations)
% DONE: r^{eq}_i -> r^*_i throughout (the equivalent allocation is an allocation of the uniform-price regime, so * is consistent); its ratio to the world average is \rho^*_i.
% TODO? give it more slowly, e.g. first 1st-order then 2nd-order?
To first order in the price gap, 
\begin{equation}
  r^*_i \approx e^A_i \approx e^*_i\Bigl[\,1+\varepsilon_i(1-\pi_i)\Bigr].
    \label{eq:req_first}
\end{equation}
To second order, with $\varepsilon_i$ evaluated at $p^*$,
\begin{equation}
  r^*_i \;\approx\; e^A_i - \frac{(e^A_i-e^*_i)^2}{2\,\varepsilon_i\,e^*_i}
  \;\approx\; e^*_i\Bigl[\,1+\varepsilon_i(1-\pi_i)-\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_i(1-\pi_i)^2\Bigr].
  \label{eq:req_second}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equating $y_i-a_i(e^*_i)+(r_i-e^*_i)p^*$ with $y_i-a_i(e^A_i)$ gives $r_i=e^*_i+[a_i(e^*_i)-a_i(e^A_i)]/p^*$. Substituting $a_i'(e^*_i)=-p^*$ into \eqref{eq:bregman} yields \eqref{eq:req_exact}. Equation \eqref{eq:req_second} follows from $e^A_i-e^*_i\approx\varepsilon_i e^*_i(1-\pi_i)$. For \eqref{eq:req_second}, note that $a_i''(e^*_i)=-1/e_i'(p^*)=p^*/(\varepsilon_i e^*_i)$, so that $b_i\approx \tfrac12\,p^*\,(e^A_i-e^*_i)^2/(\varepsilon_i e^*_i)$. 
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} shows that a differentiated price is a transfer paid in kind. Relative to the uniform price, a country allowed a lower price ($\pi_i<1$) keeps emissions $e^A_i>e^*_i$. To first order, the uniform-price regime replicates its situation by granting it exactly those emissions as rights, part of which it then sells, receiving $(e^A_i-e^*_i)p^*$. Conversely, a country asked for a higher price ($\pi_i>1$) is in the position of a country that buys rights. The size of the implicit transfer grows with the price gap, the country's emissions and the elasticity of those emissions. The same formula, read in reverse, converts any allocation of rights into an equivalent autarky price.

It is convenient to express an allocation as a multiple of the world average, $r_i=\rho_i\,\bar e$, so that $\rho_i=1$ is an equal per capita allocation. We denote by $\rho^*_i\equiv r^*_i/\bar e$ the equivalent ratio, and by $\hat\rho_i$ its first-order approximation:
\begin{equation}
  \hat\rho_i \;\equiv\; \frac{e^A_i}{\bar e},
  \label{eq:rhohat_static}
\end{equation}
country $i$'s per capita emissions under the differentiated schedule relative to the world average. By \eqref{eq:req_exact}, $\rho^*_i=\hat\rho_i-b_i/(p^*\bar e)\le\hat\rho_i$: the first-order prediction errs on the generous side. A differentiated schedule is thus, to first order, a grandfathering rule, applied to the emissions that the schedule itself induces.

\begin{proposition}[Dominance of the uniform price]\label{prop:dominance}
Allocate rights $r_i=e^A_i$ to every country. This allocation (i) is feasible at the cap of the differentiated regime, $\sum_i N_i r_i=E$; (ii) leaves every country weakly better off than under the differentiated regime, strictly so if $a_i$ is strictly convex and $p_i\neq p^*$; and (iii) leaves a surplus of $\sum_i N_i b_i\geq 0$. Expressed in emissions, the rights can be cut %by $\sum_i N_i b_i/p^*$ tonnes, 
to the equivalent allocation $(r^*_i)_i$, with no country worse off than under differentiated prices.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Part (i) holds by construction. For part (ii), the consumption gain of country $i$ is $[a_i(e^A_i)-a_i(e^*_i)]+(e^A_i-e^*_i)p^*=b_i\geq0$. For part (iii), sum these gains. 
\end{proof}

A country that is allowed a lower price saves on abatement costs. %, but at a diminishing rate. 
Under the uniform price, the same country can abate up to $p^*$ and sell the freed rights at $p^*$, which is always at least as good a deal. By Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, the surplus is of second order in the price gaps, whereas the implicit transfers are of first order. The efficiency argument for a uniform price is therefore robust but quantitatively modest when prices are not very dispersed, whereas the distributional stakes of the allocation are large. 

Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance} holds total emissions at $E=\sum_i N_i e^A_i$ and the uniform price at $p^*$: turning the surplus into a lower cap raises the price, so that in general equilibrium the equivalent rights are slightly greater than $e^A_i - b_i/p^*$. Therefore, the attainable emissions cut differs from $\sum_i N_i b_i/p^*$, which is why we solve for it numerically in Section~\ref{sec:proposals}.

Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance} is a first-best statement. It abstracts from the second-best rationales for differentiation reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:arguments}. None of these requires the absence of transfers.
What it questions is the argument that differentiation is needed because transfers would be infeasible: differentiation involves the same distributive outcome as well-chosen transfers; it only makes them implicit.

\subsection{Dynamic setting}
\label{sec:dynamic}

With several periods $t$, prices $p_{it}$, rights and emissions become trajectories, populations $N_{it}$ change over time, and $\beta_t$ denotes the discount factor. Under total utilitarianism, country $i$'s objective is $\sum_t \beta_t\,N_{it}\,u(c_{it})$: each period is weighted by the number of people alive in it, so welfare equivalence requires equal net present values of \emph{total} consumption, not of consumption per capita. It is therefore convenient to state the dynamic results in totals rather than per capita: $R_{it}\equiv N_{it}r_{it}$ and $E_{it}\equiv N_{it}e_{it}$ are country $i$'s rights and emissions in period $t$, $E^A_{it}$ its emissions under the differentiated regime, and $E_t=\sum_i E_{it}$ world emissions, so that the population weights are carried by the quantities themselves.

Only under a Hotelling price path, the natural benchmark with a fixed carbon budget and efficient capital markets, does the price weighting drop out, and \eqref{eq:rhohat_hotelling} then states the familiar claim that what a differentiated schedule is worth to a country is the share of the world carbon budget it takes, measured against its share of world population.

Equation~\eqref{eq:rhohat_dyn} is the first-order approximation that we take to the model. It ignores three channels that NICE2020 includes: the higher-order terms due to the convexity of abatement costs, the effect of transfers on capital accumulation, and the incidence of prices and transfers within countries.

\paragraph{Fair allocations cannot be replicated by differentiated prices.} The converse of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} does not hold. Every price schedule has an equivalent allocation, but not every allocation has an equivalent non-negative price. Even at a zero price, a country cannot emit more than its unabated emissions, so the most that differentiation can offer it is the allocation $\rho_i(0)$ at which its curve meets the vertical axis. For low emitters, that bound lies far below an equal per capita share: 0.40 for Nigeria and 0.07 for the DRC. Allocations that give such countries an equal per capita share or more --- as equal per capita rights, historical responsibility or capacity to pay would --- could only be replicated by paying them to emit. Differentiated prices can lighten the burden of low-income countries, but they cannot turn it into the net gain that these allocations imply. This is consistent with \citet{le_moigne_distributional_2026}, who find that income-differentiated price floors do not necessarily yield fairer outcomes than a uniform price combined with international transfers. It also explains why the equivalent allocations of the schedules studied in Section~\ref{sec:proposals} are so unequal.

\subsection{Proposals}

We feed three proposals into the model as country-level price schedules.

\citet{wolfram_building_2025} form a coalition of 47 countries\footnote{The members are Algeria, Australia, Brazil, Cameroon, Canada, China, Egypt, the European Union, Ghana, Iceland, India, Indonesia, Kenya, Liechtenstein, Mozambique, Norway, Switzerland, Thailand, Togo, the United Kingdom, Uganda and Zambia.} including China, the EU, the UK, India and Brazil, which together emit 48\% of world CO$_2$ in 2030. Members apply \$75, \$50 and \$25/t in high-, upper-middle- and lower-income countries, the tiers of the IMF's international carbon price floor \citep{parry_proposal_2021}. The proposal targets four emissions-intensive industries, whereas NICE2020 has no sectoral detail. We apply the floors economy-wide, so our scenario is more ambitious than the one the authors model.

\citet{banerjee_grand_2025} ask \$10, \$30 and \$50/t of low-, lower-middle- and upper-middle-income countries, in exchange for the compensation of climate damages, with revenues retained domestically. High-income countries fund the compensation and are expected to price carbon heavily, but the proposal gives no figure. Rather than leaving them unpriced, we price them at \$75/t, the top tier of \citet{wolfram_building_2025}.

\citet{equal_right_climate_2023} propose a global cap on fossil fuel extraction, shrinking by 10\% a year to stay within 1.6\textdegree C, with national allowances allocated per capita. Each country sells its extraction licences at a price floor graduated by income group (\$240, \$120, \$60 and \$30/t) and discounted by up to 60\% for climate vulnerability, which gives floors ranging from \$12 to \$240/t in 2025 (\$144/t on average), rising by about 16\% a year. The revenues are pooled in a global fund that pays climate grants and a universal dividend. We use only the schedule of price floors, which is far more dispersed than the other two, and treat it like them, with revenues retained domestically: our results for this schedule therefore do not evaluate the Equal Right proposal as a whole, which already involves large explicit transfers.

\subsection{Design}

Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} holds at a given cap. We therefore compare each proposal with a \emph{club} regime. Its members are the countries the proposal prices, and they face one uniform price $p^*_t$, calibrated so that their emissions equal those under the proposal. This price is \$49.2/t \citep{wolfram_building_2025}, \$55.8/t \citep{banerjee_grand_2025} and \$138.5/t (Equal Right) in 2030, and then grows by 4--5\% a year for the first two. Non-members are unpriced in both regimes. Member $i$ receives rights $r_{it}=\rho_i\,\bar e_t$ per capita, where $\bar e_t$ is the club's emissions per capita under the proposal, so that $\rho_i=1$ is an equal per capita allocation.

We solve for the equivalent ratios $\rho^*_i$ on two criteria: welfare (the NPV of population-weighted EDE consumption) or total consumption. Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance} implies that the club regime leaves a surplus, which we use in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Reduced emissions}: rights are cut until every member is exactly as well off as under the proposal. The rights add up to the cap, and the club price rises to deliver it. The surplus takes the form of lower emissions. As in Propositions~\ref{prop:equiv} and~\ref{prop:dominance}, members are compared at the same damages: while solving for the allocation, we hold each country's damages at the level implied by the proposal's own temperature path. This is an accounting device rather than a scenario --- emissions do fall, and the avoided damages are real --- and it is what isolates the efficiency gain of uniform pricing. Left to respond, the avoided damages would finance further cuts on their own, and the solve would tighten the cap until the club price reached the club's own social cost of carbon, which measures the ambition of the schedule rather than the instrument used to deliver it (Online Appendix~\ref{app:variants}). The emissions cut we report is therefore the surplus of Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance} expressed in tonnes: the abatement that the efficiency gain pays for, at the equilibrium price path. The avoided damages are reported separately, as the fall in 2100 warming and in the world welfare gain, both computed with damages endogenous.
  \item \emph{Increased consumption}: the club's emissions are kept at their level under the proposal in every year, and the surplus rights are shared so as to maximise the smallest relative gain of any member, which equalises the gains of all members.
\end{itemize}
We solve both problems jointly for all members. At each iteration, the allocation sets the cap, the club price is recalibrated to deliver it, and each member's ratio is updated by a secant step on its gap to the target. The solve stops when no member's gap exceeds 0.002\% of its proposal level (reduced emissions) or when members' gains differ by less than that (increased consumption). In the Online Appendix, we also report an \emph{isolated} solve, which finds each country's ratio in turn while every other member is granted its own emissions under the proposal. It is the partial-equilibrium analogue of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, but it credits each country with the whole efficiency gain of joining the market, a gain that cannot be paid to all members at once. With the isolated solve, the surplus is taken either as reduced emissions, by applying the isolated ratios together, or as increased consumption, by scaling them up uniformly to the proposal's emissions. The Online Appendix also reports two alternative ways of sharing the surplus at the proposal's emissions: in proportion to population times marginal utility, and by uniform scaling.

\paragraph{The dividend: fewer emissions.} Replacing the Banerjee et al.\ schedule with the club price and the equivalent allocation, and cutting the allocation until every member is exactly as well off as under the schedule, reduces the club's emissions by 4.7\% and its 2100 warming by 0.019\textdegree C. This is the dividend of Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance}: the same consumption for every member, with less warming for all. The solve holds each member within 0.002\% of its level under the schedule, and one of the 179 members ends more than 0.005\% below it: Mongolia, which the model makes a beneficiary of warming, so that the avoided damages of a tighter cap are a cost to it (Online Appendix~\ref{app:variants}). The corresponding cuts are 5.1\% for \citet{wolfram_building_2025} and 15.2\% for the Equal Right schedule.

\paragraph{The dividend: more consumption.} The alternative is to keep the club's emissions at their level under the proposal and to share the surplus rights so as to raise the smallest gain, which equalises the gains of all members. Every member of the Banerjee et al.\ club then gains 0.052\% of the net present value of its consumption, and world consumption rises by 0.053\%. Again, no member loses, by construction, and the gains are of the same order as the emissions dividend measured in money.

\paragraph{The dividend grows with price dispersion.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, the dividend is of second order in the price gaps. The gains above are small because the two schedules span a moderate range (\$10--75/t in 2030) that brackets the club price. The Equal Right schedule, which grades prices by climate vulnerability as well as by income, ranges from \$12 to \$240/t. Run with the same 5\% growth rate, it yields an emissions cut of 15.2\% and a gain of 0.180\% for every member, three and a half times the gain under the Banerjee et al.\ schedule. The cost of differentiation is thus negligible for mild dispersion but grows quickly as prices diverge --- and the dispersion of a schedule is not a detail of implementation: it is what makes it redistributive.

\paragraph{Welfare or consumption.} Solving the equivalence on welfare rather than on consumption changes individual allocations by up to half an equal per capita share (the United States under Banerjee et al.: 2.46 instead of 2.92). Matching welfare takes fewer rights for these schedules, because moving a high-priced member down to the club price also compresses its within-country distribution, which the inequality-averse measure counts as part of the compensation, while total consumption does not. Since that channel depends on the model's assumptions about the domestic incidence of carbon prices, and since the question of this paper is between-country, total consumption is the more transparent criterion; both are reported throughout (Online Appendix~\ref{app:ede}).

\paragraph{Isolated and joint solves.} The allocations above are solved jointly for all members. Solving each country in turn instead, with every other member granted its own emissions under the proposal, is the partial-equilibrium analogue of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}, but it credits each country with the entire efficiency gain of joining the market. Applied to all members at once, those allocations leave 24 of 47 Wolfram et al.\ members and 36 of 179 Banerjee et al.\ members below their proposal consumption, whereas the joint allocation leaves none and one respectively. For policy design, the relevant allocation is therefore the joint one (Online Appendix~\ref{app:variants}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

Differentiating carbon prices by income is increasingly proposed as a way to reconcile carbon pricing with equity. This paper questions the premise of differentiation that cross-border transfers are politically infeasible. A differentiated schedule is equivalent, to first order, to a uniform price with tradable rights that grandfather each country on the emissions the schedule itself induces. These are transfers of the same first-order size, only paid in (excess or forgone) abatement rather than in money. 
Moreover, since abatement costs are convex, the explicit version leaves every country weakly better off at the same cap.

% The quantitative analysis supports each step of the argument. The closed-form allocation explains nearly all of the cross-country variation in the equivalent rights solved in a model with capital accumulation, within-country inequality and general-equilibrium price adjustment. 
Replacing the differentiated prices of \citet{wolfram_building_2025} or \citet{banerjee_grand_2025} with a uniform price at equivalent rights would cut the coalition's emissions by about 5\% % TODO: correct figure
% DONE: the figure was the old joint solve's (and quoted global rather than coalition emissions). The joint solves under total utilitarianism give 5.1\% (Wolfram et al.) and 4.7\% (Banerjee, Duflo and Greenstone) of the coalition's emissions on the consumption criterion, 4.9\% and 4.3\% on welfare, and 15.2\% for the far more dispersed Equal Right schedule at 5\%/yr (18.8\% on its own escalation path). Since both coalitions are defined by the proposals themselves (47 countries covering 48\% of world CO2 for Wolfram et al., the whole world for Banerjee et al.), the sentence now says "the coalition's emissions".
while leaving every member as well off as under the schedule, and by 15\% under the more dispersed schedule of \citet{equal_right_climate_2023}. Alternatively, at unchanged emissions, it would raise every member's consumption, by 0.02 to 0.18\% in net present value.

The equivalence runs only one way. Every schedule of prices has an equivalent allocation of rights, but no schedule of non-negative prices can deliver to a low emitter what a generous allocation would: even a zero price leaves Nigeria below two fifths of an equal per capita share, and the DRC below a tenth. Allocations based on equal per capita rights, historical responsibility or capacity to pay are therefore out of reach of price differentiation, however finely it is graded.

Two implications follow for negotiations. First, the equivalence formula provides an exchange rate between a price and emission rights, allowing parties to shift the discussion from one notion to the other as needed. %can negotiate over one and implement the other, so that equity and efficiency are separated as the textbook recommends. 
Second, the value of national sovereignty invoked to dismiss an international cap-and-trade should be balanced with the efficiency gains that an international commitment would enable. 
% Second, since the implicit and explicit transfers are of the same size, the political objection to transfers must be an objection to their visibility, not to their magnitude. %Whether visibility helps or hinders an agreement is an open empirical question. 
Furthermore, survey evidence shows %that large explicit North-to-South transfers are actually popular even in high-income countries and indicates 
that 85\% of worldwide respondents state that climate policy at the \textit{global} level is needed, at odd with the supposed taste for national sovereignty \citep{fabre_majority_2025}. Finally, robust majority acceptance of an international cap-and-trade funding equal per capita cash transfers indicates that most people are ready to accept large North--South transfers that differentiated autarky prices cannot deliver.

The analysis has limitations. NICE2020 has no trade, so it cannot capture leakage or the terms-of-trade motives for differentiation. It has no sectoral detail, which forced us to apply sectoral proposals economy-wide and ignores the country-specific costs of sectoral reallocation. It also assumes perfect enforcement of both regimes. The proposals' price paths after 2030 are our assumption. Extending the equivalence to settings with trade and different sectors, where a uniform price must coexist with border adjustments, is a natural next step to better inform climate negotiators.

\section{Model details}
\label{app:model}

\paragraph{Configuration.} All runs use NICE2020 with country-level revenue recycling in proportion to $c^{\eta}$, computed once from a uniform-price reference run and held fixed across runs, the default income elasticity of emissions across deciles, emissions measured on a consumption basis (carbon footprints), and international transfers entering national income, hence investment. The same configuration is used in the differentiated and in the uniform-price regime, so that the two regimes differ only in prices and rights.

\paragraph{Rest-of-world price in the autarky scenario.} When country $i$ applies $\pi_i p^*_t$, the rest of the world applies a price $p_{-i,t}$ such that global emissions follow the path of the uniform-price run. Since every country abates at the rate $\mu=(p/p^{\mathrm{back}}_t)^{1/(\theta_2-1)}$, with $\theta_2=2.6$, the rest of the world's emissions are $B_{-i,t}(1-\mu_{-i,t})$, where $B_{-i,t}$ is its baseline. We therefore update $p_{-i,t}$ by Newton steps in the abatement rate, $\mu_{-i,t}\leftarrow 1-(E_t-E_{it})/B_{-i,t}$, re-evaluating $B_{-i,t}$ in each run, until world emissions are within 0.01\% of the target in every year.

\paragraph{Rights of the rest of the world in the uniform-price scenario.} Country $i$ receives $\rho_i$ times its population share of global emissions in each year. The remainder is distributed to other countries in proportion to their emissions, which keeps total rights equal to the cap for any $\rho_i$. When country $i$'s rights exceed the cap, the remainder is negative and the rest of the world pays for its emissions.

\paragraph{Abatement beyond 100\%.} Standard implementations constrain the abatement rate to $\mu\in[0,1]$. Under high autarky prices, country $i$'s price exceeds the backstop price and this constraint binds. In the autarky scenario we extrapolate the cost function beyond $\mu=1$, which represents carbon dioxide removal at rising marginal cost. The proposals of Section~\ref{sec:proposals} are instead capped at the backstop price.

\paragraph{Viability flags.} We flag grid points at which (i) the rest-of-world price has to be negative in some year to keep the global path, and (ii) country $i$'s rights exceed global emissions in some year, leaving the rest of the world with negative rights. Only the second occurs, for China and India at allocations of six equal per capita shares or more (the crosses in Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps}). The first never does: the lowest rest-of-world price over the whole grid (after 2025, when the benchmark itself is still zero) is \$3.4/t, for China at $\pi_i=4.75$ in 2026. Even when a large emitter prices carbon at nearly five times the benchmark, holding world emissions on the benchmark path only requires the rest of the world to price carbon less, never to pay for emissions. The figures this replaces flagged such points for some countries, but on a different quantity: the condition was evaluated on the linear first guess for the rest-of-world price, whose emission shares are divided by a world total that approaches zero late in the century.

\section{Solves, sharing rules and numerical accuracy}
\label{app:variants}

\paragraph{The two solves.} The \emph{joint} solve of the main text finds all $\rho_i$ at once. At each iteration the allocation sets the cap, the club price is recalibrated to deliver it, and each member's ratio is moved by a secant step on its gap to the target. For the reduced-emissions variant, an outer loop then moves the level of the allocation until the members' common gain is zero, so that every member ends exactly at its level under the schedule. The \emph{isolated} solve instead finds each country's ratio in turn, with every other member granted its own emissions under the schedule and the club price held at the value that reproduces the schedule's emissions. It is the partial-equilibrium counterpart of Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv}: it ignores that the other members also change their allocation, and that the club price adjusts to the resulting cap.

The two solves agree on the aggregate. Taking the equivalent allocation at face value cuts the Wolfram et al.\ club's emissions by 6.9\% (isolated) against 6.9\% (joint), and the Banerjee et al.\ club's by 6.7\% against 6.0\%. They differ on the distribution, and the difference is systematic: the isolated solve credits each country in turn with the whole efficiency gain of joining the market, a gain that cannot be paid to every member at once. Applied together, the isolated allocations leave 25 of 47 Wolfram et al.\ members and 31 of 179 Banerjee et al.\ members below their level under the schedule, while the joint allocation leaves none. Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_isolated} reports the isolated allocations in the format of Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_main}.

\paragraph{Holding damages fixed.} In the reduced-emissions variant, the cap is tighter than the schedule's, so warming is lower and every country's damages fall. Propositions~\ref{prop:equiv} and~\ref{prop:dominance} compare the two regimes at the same damages, and so does our solve: when searching for the allocation that leaves each member as well off as under the schedule, we hold each country's damages at the level implied by the schedule's own temperature path. Emissions still fall, and the avoided damages are real; they are simply not allowed to pay for the cut that the solve is measuring. The device is the numerical counterpart of the statement, in Proposition~\ref{prop:dominance}, that the cap can be tightened by $\sum_i N_i b_i/p^*$ tonnes with no country worse off: that statement too changes emissions while treating damages as given, and is to be read as a conversion of the surplus into tonnes rather than as a description of a world.

Letting damages respond instead answers a different question. The solve then keeps tightening the cap for as long as the club's avoided damages exceed its marginal abatement cost, that is, until the club price reaches the club's own social cost of carbon. Its answer is the club's optimal cap, which depends on the ambition of climate policy rather than on the instrument that delivers it; in a preliminary solve for \citet{banerjee_grand_2025}, whose club is the whole world, the cap had fallen by 21\% and every member was still better off when the solve was stopped. We therefore do not report it. The avoided damages that the tighter cap does deliver are reported separately, as the fall in 2100 warming and as the world welfare gain of the variant, both computed with damages endogenous.

\paragraph{Alternative sharing rules.} The increased-consumption variant of the main text shares the surplus so as to maximise the smallest relative gain, which equalises the gains of all members. Two other rules are reported in Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights}. Sharing the surplus in proportion to population times marginal utility directs it to the poorest members: it yields by far the largest inequality-averse gain (a world welfare gain of 0.231\% for Banerjee et al., against 0.054\% under the maximin rule) but, unlike the maximin rule, it leaves 11 of 179 members below their consumption under the schedule. Uniform scaling of the whole allocation is dominated: the extra rights go to the largest holders, and the looser cap lowers the club price, which devalues the rights of small, generously endowed net sellers. It leaves no member below on consumption, but its inequality-averse gain is the smallest of the three rules (0.039\% for Banerjee et al.), and 9 of 47 Wolfram et al.\ members and 64 of 179 Banerjee et al.\ members end below their welfare under the schedule.

\paragraph{Who loses from the avoided damages.} The equivalence is solved with damages held at the schedule's temperature path, and every member is then within 0.001\% of indifference. The gains reported in Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_main} are computed with damages endogenous, so they also carry the avoided damages of the tighter cap, which are shared in proportion to each country's marginal damage $\beta_1+2\beta_2 T$. In the Kalkuhl--Wenz specification of NICE2020, 13 countries have $\beta_1<0$ and a local anomaly below $-\beta_1/2\beta_2$, so their marginal damage is negative: they gain from warming, and less of it makes them worse off. They are the only members that end below their level under the schedule --- Mongolia under \citet{banerjee_grand_2025}, and Finland, Iceland, Mongolia and Russia under Equal Right at 5\%/yr, ordered by $|\beta_1|$ as the mechanism implies, and none under \citet{wolfram_building_2025}, whose cap moves warming least. The loss is a mirror image of the gain: Nigeria and India, whose marginal damage is largest, gain 0.23\% and 0.18\% of their consumption from the same avoided warming, against Mongolia's $-0.05\%$. This is a property of the damage function, not of the equivalence: at the damages of the schedule, on which the equivalence is defined, no member loses.

\paragraph{Numerical accuracy.} The club price is calibrated so that members' emissions match the cap within 0.01\% of the club's peak emissions in every year. The joint solves stop when no member's gap to its target exceeds 0.002\% of its level under the schedule, and the reduced-emissions outer loop when the common gain is below 0.001\%. A member is counted as losing when it falls short by more than 0.005\%, four times the tolerance. Prices apply from 2025, while welfare is the net present value over 2030--2100, so what happens in the five first years enters the objective only through its effect on capital accumulation. Measured on the Wolfram et al.\ allocation, this does not flatter the dividend: read over 2025--2100 instead, members are on average 0.029\% \emph{above} their level under the schedule, so the reported cut is, in the aggregate, conservative. It does affect the distribution across members, who range from $-0.16\%$ to $+0.14\%$ on that longer window against $-0.0003\%$ to $+0.0008\%$ on the window solved. An equivalence solved on 2025--2100 would therefore give the same aggregate dividend and a somewhat different allocation.

\section{Welfare or consumption, and the Equal Right schedule}
\label{app:ede}

Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_main} reports every allocation twice: solved so that each member keeps its welfare (the net present value of its equally-distributed-equivalent consumption) and solved so that it keeps its total consumption (both population-weighted). The two can move in opposite directions for the same country, for a within-country reason. Carbon costs fall on deciles in proportion to income raised to an elasticity below one, which is regressive, and revenue is returned in proportion to $c^{\eta}$, which is regressive too. More domestic pricing therefore widens within-country inequality. A member held exactly at its total consumption can thus gain or lose on the inequality-averse measure, and conversely. Because this channel depends on the model's assumptions about the domestic incidence of carbon prices, while the question of this paper is between-country, the main text reads the consumption column as the benchmark and reports the welfare column alongside it.

The Equal Right schedule is designed to rise by about 16\% a year, far faster than the 5\% we assume for the other two. Table~\ref{tab:equiv_rights_equalright} reports it on both paths. On its own path, the schedule pushes most members to full abatement by 2050 and reaches 1.48\textdegree C of warming; its club price is far above the other schedules', and the ranking of the welfare- and consumption-equivalent allocations reverses. The dividend is larger still: replacing it with the club price and the equivalent allocation cuts the club's emissions by 18.8\%, against 15.2\% on the 5\%-a-year path. Its effect on 2100 warming is smaller ($-0.013$\textdegree C against $-0.037$\textdegree C), because a schedule that has already driven emissions to zero by mid-century leaves little room for a further cut in cumulative terms.
